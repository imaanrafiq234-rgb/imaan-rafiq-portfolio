Child safety, materials and circular economy

    • Anything that goes into a child's mouth carries a responsibility most products do not. Material choice is not a styling decision here; it is the whole product.

    I specified silicone plastic — heat resistant, water repellent and flexible, already trusted across children's products and medical devices in the UK. No harsh plastics, no toxic additives, nothing that risks a child's health for the sake of a lower unit cost.

    Design considerations

    The concept was designed around real pacifier problems, not just the look of the product.

    • —Dependency — some babies become overly dependent, making weaning hard. Adding a teether lets the baby reduce pain and slowly come off it.
    • —Nipple confusion — introducing a pacifier too early can make latching difficult, so a suitable age range has to be considered.
    • —Sleep disruption — a pacifier can become a sleep association, so if it falls out at night the baby wakes. This drives the soft, easily replaced form.
    • —Hygiene — pacifiers accumulate dirt, saliva and bacteria, so the shell is shaped for straightforward cleaning and sterilising.

    United States point of difference

    The Cocomelon-inspired shell has soft, distinguishable features so the child recognises it and wants to use it. Colour choices are not random: green for calm, cream for warmth and nostalgia, red to catch a parent's eye on the shelf, and blue to signal trust and safety — colours already associated with children's safety products in the UK.

    A lip and groove construction lets the shell and mouthpiece assemble cleanly for injection moulding, keeping the design manufacturable at scale.

    Engineering drawings allow the product to be mass produced with sufficient and exact results via injection moulding.

    Material: silicone plastic

    Silicone is a synthetic polymer of silicon, oxygen, carbon and hydrogen. It is used in kitchen utensils, medical devices and sealants, and differs from traditional plastics because it stays flexible and heat resistant.

    It cannot be easily recycled — it undergoes complex methods including melting and reshaping, typically broken down or ground into a powder, and can be reused as a filler for other products. Because it is not as established as other plastics, silicone needs specialised machinery. Cost sits around £2–£10 depending on the retailer and design.

    Wastage and the circular economy

    Wastage and the circular economy were just as important as safety. Silicone is not the easiest material to recycle, but it holds up to repeated sterilising and reuse far longer than cheaper alternatives, which cuts down how often a parent needs to replace it.

    I chose this design because I believe it best aids the future generation into a circular economy — the mothers who need these pacifiers will buy them and recommend them, and the product can be injection moulded at ease and mass produced with intricate detail if needed.
