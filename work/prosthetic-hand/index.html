    • Continuing the sketching series — this time a prosthetic hand designed for use in a kitchen environment.

    The early sketches started with form and concept analysis, studying how a hand actually grips, holds and manipulates a knife or utensil. A prosthetic hand for a chef has to move the way a working hand moves, not just look like one.

    From sketches to constraints

    From there the sketches got more technical. I annotated where the circuit could sit on top of the prosthetic arm rather than the palm, since that is the surface a chef uses constantly and the electronics would be damaged.

    I worked out how many motion pins the fingers needed, and flagged early that the strings pulling the fingers had to be robust enough not to get accidentally cut by a knife mid-use. Even the aesthetic of the wiring got sketched out — not flashy, blending into a professional kitchen environment rather than standing out.

    One sketch pushed the concept further with a seasoning dispenser built into the fingers, pouring straight into a shot measure — designed specifically around how a chef works, not just a generic hand replacement.

    The build

    A flex sensor attached to the user's arm reads movement and sends the coding to an Arduino board, which sends it into the servo motors pulling the hand simulation — the same string and pulley logic from the early sketches.

    A temperature sensor in the fingertips triggers an LED and vibration motor: red for hot and blue for cold, so the user gets a warning before contact with something dangerous. The arm itself sits inside a silicone dome for protection.

    Why the sketching stage mattered

    None of that would have come together without the sketching stage first. Every constraint — where the circuit goes, how the fingers move, what a chef actually needs from their hand — got worked out on paper before a single part was made.
